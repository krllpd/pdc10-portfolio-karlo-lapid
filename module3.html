Module 3

Conversational UX

A short summary of what this module covered and the main idea you took from it.

3.1 The Broken Bot Labyrinth

Goal

As a team, find what makes a broken campus chatbot frustrating to use. In each level we named the conversational UX problem, explained why it hurts the user, and rewrote a better bot response, ending with a Final Boss that combined several problems at once.

Game evidence

Reflection

This game showed me that a chatbot can be technically correct and still be bad to talk to. In Level 1, the bot answered “I need to fix my enrollment” by asking for ten things at once, including the student’s birthday and address. Our fix was to ask one simple question first and only request what is actually needed.

The Final Boss mixed everything together: too much information requested at once, sensitive data it didn’t need, three problems dumped together, a useless “contact IT” handoff, the bot taking actions it shouldn’t, and a cold tone. Breaking it down one issue at a time made it manageable.

My biggest takeaway is that good conversational design means thinking like the user: ask one thing at a time, explain why you need information, stay friendly, and know when to hand off to a real person.

3.2 The Prompt Vault: Operation Six Locks

Goal

As a team, escape the Prompt Vault by opening six locks. Each lock required a different prompting technique, and the Game Master tested every prompt against hidden criteria. Each opened lock gave a letter fragment, and the six fragments formed the final exit code.

Game Master evidence

Reflection

Because the Game Master’s criteria were hidden, we couldn’t just guess what it wanted. We had to make each prompt clear and complete on its own. Lock 1 cleared on the first attempt because our prompt defined all four groups in plain words and set an exact output format.

Collecting the fragments P, R, O, M, P and T to spell PROMPT was a good reminder of what the whole game was about: the quality of the answer depends on the quality of the prompt.

My biggest takeaway is to write prompts as if the AI knows nothing about what I want. Spelling out the task, the categories and the format leaves less room for mistakes.
